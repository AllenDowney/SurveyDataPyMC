# Survey Gender Identity Measurement: Summary

## Project Goal

This project researches and compares how different large-scale surveys operationalize and ask about gender identity. The goal is to create a systematic comparison of question wording, response options, and methodological approaches across a diverse range of survey organizations (research, government, and NGO), with a focus on evaluating how well different methods measure gender identity accurately and inclusively.

## Surveys Reviewed

We reviewed 11 major surveys across different organizational types:

### Surveys That Collect Gender Identity Information

1. **General Social Survey (GSS)** - Two-step approach (sex assigned at birth + current gender identity)
2. **American National Election Studies (ANES)** - Modified two-step approach (self-reported sex + gender identity + direct transgender question)
3. **Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS)** - Optional Gender Identity module with transgender question
4. **National Health Interview Survey (NHIS)** - Experimental gender identity questions (three-step approach)
5. **American Community Survey (ACS)** - Testing Gender Identity questions for 2027 implementation

### Surveys That Collect Only Binary Sex/Gender

6. **Current Population Survey (CPS)** - Binary sex question only
7. **Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS)** - Structural approach (gender embedded through separate questionnaires)
8. **Multiple Indicator Cluster Surveys (MICS)** - Structural approach (similar to DHS)
9. **World Values Survey (WVS)** - Interviewer-coded binary sex variable
10. **European Social Survey (ESS)** - Binary gender variable (includes sexual orientation in self-completion, but no gender identity)
11. **Afrobarometer** - Interviewer-recorded binary gender variable

## Evaluation Criteria

We developed seven criteria for evaluating Gender Identity measurement methods:

1. **Classification Accuracy:** Collected data makes it possible to correctly classify cis men and women and trans men and women, and others who don't consider themselves to be in any of those categories.

2. **Error Checking:** Questions should include error checking to minimize error rates. Because cis people are a large majority, if even 1% of them are misclassified, the number of misclassified cis people could be comparable to the number of actual trans people.

3. **Comprehensibility:** Questions should be understandable to all respondents, regardless of education level, language proficiency, or familiarity with gender identity concepts.

4. **Response Options Coverage:** All respondents should be able to choose a response that reflects their identity accurately.

5. **Clarity of Intent:** All respondents should be confident they are answering as intended—they should not have to infer intent.

6. **Respectful Wording:** Questions should use respectful, inclusive language that acknowledges the dignity of all respondents.

7. **Non-Partisan Language:** Questions should be framed neutrally to avoid triggering political or ideological responses.

## Results Summary

### Surveys with Gender Identity Questions

#### General Social Survey (GSS)

**Approach:** Two-step Gender Identity (sex assigned at birth + current gender identity)

**Evaluation Summary:**
- **Classification Accuracy:** Partial - can classify cis and some trans people, but limited for non-binary
- **Error Checking:** Poor - no explicit error checking mechanisms
- **Comprehensibility:** Moderate - "transgender" as a category may be unclear
- **Response Options Coverage:** Partial - "None of these" is vague
- **Clarity of Intent:** Moderate - undocumented recode logic creates uncertainty
- **Respectful Wording:** Good - generally respectful language
- **Non-Partisan Language:** Good - neutral, factual language

**Key Issues:** No error checking, undocumented recode logic, "transgender" as standalone category may confuse respondents

#### American National Election Studies (ANES)

**Approach:** Modified two-step GI (self-reported sex + gender identity + direct transgender question)

**Evaluation Summary:**
- **Classification Accuracy:** Good - can classify all five groups
- **Error Checking:** Moderate - direct transgender question provides cross-check, but no confirmation for cis people
- **Comprehensibility:** Good - clear questions with familiar terminology
- **Response Options Coverage:** Good - comprehensive options including "Nonbinary" and "Something else"
- **Clarity of Intent:** Good - explicit documentation that transgender status is not inferred
- **Respectful Wording:** Good - uses "Man/Woman" and respectful terminology
- **Non-Partisan Language:** Good - neutral, factual language

**Key Strengths:** Explicit nonbinary option, direct transgender question prevents misclassification, clear documentation

#### Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS)

**Approach:** Optional GI module (transgender question with subcategories)

**Evaluation Summary:**
- **Classification Accuracy:** Moderate - can classify some groups, but limitations
- **Error Checking:** Moderate - interviewer clarification note helps, but no confirmation question
- **Comprehensibility:** Good - clear with helpful interviewer clarification note
- **Response Options Coverage:** Moderate - good for trans people, limited for non-binary
- **Clarity of Intent:** Good - direct question, clear purpose
- **Respectful Wording:** Good - respectful, descriptive terminology
- **Non-Partisan Language:** Good - neutral, health-focused language

**Key Issues:** Optional module creates state-level variation, some non-binary people may not identify as "transgender"

#### National Health Interview Survey (NHIS)

**Approach:** Experimental three-step approach (current gender → sex assigned at birth → confirmation question)

**Evaluation Summary:**
- **Classification Accuracy:** Good - can classify all five groups
- **Error Checking:** Good - includes confirmation question when responses differ
- **Comprehensibility:** Good - clear questions with explicit wording
- **Response Options Coverage:** Good - comprehensive including "Nonbinary" and "Another gender"
- **Clarity of Intent:** Good - three-step approach with confirmation makes purpose clear
- **Respectful Wording:** Good - uses "assigned at birth" and respectful terminology
- **Non-Partisan Language:** Good - neutral, health-focused language

**Key Strengths:** Confirmation question addresses critical error checking need, comprehensive coverage, clear intent

**Key Limitation:** Experimental status, not in public-use files (Research Data Center only)

#### American Community Survey (ACS)

**Approach:** Testing two-step GI questions (planned implementation 2027)

**Evaluation Summary:**
- **All Criteria:** To be determined - questions still in testing phase
- Proposed approach similar to GSS (sex assigned at birth + current gender identity)
- Policy context (Executive Order 14168) may affect final implementation

### Surveys with Binary Sex/Gender Only

The following surveys collect only binary sex or gender information and do not include gender identity questions:

- **Current Population Survey (CPS)** - Binary sex question ("What is [NAME]'s sex?" - Male/Female)
- **Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS)** - Structural approach (gender embedded through separate questionnaires and eligibility rules)
- **Multiple Indicator Cluster Surveys (MICS)** - Structural approach (similar to DHS)
- **World Values Survey (WVS)** - Interviewer-coded binary sex variable (field label: "Sex (of respondent)")
- **European Social Survey (ESS)** - Binary gender variable ("Are you male or female?" - includes sexual orientation in self-completion, but no gender identity)
- **Afrobarometer** - Interviewer-recorded binary gender variable (Q101: "Respondent's gender" - Male/Female)

## Key Findings

### Best Practices

1. **NHIS's confirmation question** is a model for error checking - it asks respondents to confirm when responses differ, helping prevent misclassification of cis people.

2. **ANES's explicit nonbinary option** and direct transgender question provide comprehensive coverage and prevent misclassification.

3. **Clear documentation** (like ANES's explicit statement that transgender status is not inferred) helps both respondents and researchers understand intent.

### Common Issues

1. **Lack of error checking** - Most surveys lack confirmation questions or consistency checks, creating risk of misclassification.

2. **Undocumented recode logic** - GSS's derived SEX variable has undocumented recode logic, creating uncertainty about classification accuracy.

3. **Vague response options** - "None of these" or "Something else" without specification may not provide adequate coverage.

4. **Terminology confusion** - Using "transgender" as a standalone category (GSS) may confuse respondents who don't understand it as a gender identity.

### Recommendations

1. **Include error checking mechanisms** - Confirmation questions or consistency checks are essential to minimize misclassification.

2. **Provide explicit nonbinary options** - Rather than relying on "None of these" or "Something else," surveys should include explicit "Nonbinary" options.

3. **Document recode logic** - All derived variables should have clearly documented recode logic.

4. **Use clear terminology** - Avoid using "transgender" as a standalone gender category; instead use direct questions about transgender identity (like ANES) or provide clearer options.

5. **Ensure comprehensibility** - Questions should be tested for understanding across diverse populations.
